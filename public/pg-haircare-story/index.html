      Four themes came out of the visits.

      Routine, time pressure, product feel, and storage. We turned those into what the package has to do, and how the routine should feel.

      People compared dispensers and placed them in the routine.

      The process in detail

      The full case study

      Six phases, in the order they happened.

      01 · Context

      The brief, and how the work was organised

      P&G sponsored a Northwestern EDI team to explore packaging for a conditioning product.

      We connected consumer research with product handling, dispensing, and everyday haircare routines.

      The project ran as four sprints across two settings. The first two were in people’s homes and set the questions. The second two were at a central test site, where people tried early studies.

      The final design, the brand, and the product name are not shown on this page.

      02 · Research

      Eight in-home visits

      We wrote screeners to find people with a hair-care routine, then visited eight homes and observed the routine and the objects kept nearby.

      1. ScreenerWritten first, to find people with a hair-care routine.
      2. Discussion guideA shared set of questions for every visit.
      3. Scavenger huntParticipants found and showed us objects from their routine.
      4. Collage homeworkA collage participants made about their routine.

      What it changed

      The first central-site round compared dispenser use, counter and storage choices, and the steps of the routine.

      03 · Synthesis

      From observations to needs to test questions

      Back in the studio we grouped what we had seen and mapped the routine. The themes became needs, and the needs became the questions we took to the test site.

      What we kept seeing

      • The routine
      • Time pressure
      • Product feel
      • Storage

      What people need

      Functional

      • Handling
      • Application
      • Storage
      • Steps in the routine

      Emotional

      • Control
      • Care
      • Trust
      • How the routine feels

      What we tested

      • Dispensing and application
      • Handling
      • Shelf cues

      What it changed

      Dispensing, handling, and shelf cues were tested as separate questions, each with its own activity.

      04 · Exploration

      Notes, then paper, then printed forms

      1. Visit notes

        Observations from the home visits set the questions.

      2. Paper studies

        Sketches let the team compare early forms quickly.

      3. Printed forms

        3D-printed studies made handling easy to compare.

      What it changed

      Physical studies went into people’s hands at the central site.

      05 · Testing

      Two central-site rounds

      Round 1 compared dispenser use, counter and storage choices, and routine steps. Round 2 tested dispenser studies, handling, and shelf cues.

      Three questions, each with its own activity

      Dispensing and application
      Task
      Try generic dispensing studies on mounted hair swatches.
      We observed
      Control, coverage, and the steps needed to apply product.
      Sample prompt
      “Show how you would use this during your routine.”
      Handling
      Task
      Pick up the physical form studies.
      We observed
      Grip, balance, surface feel, and ease of use.
      Sample prompt
      “Pick up the one you would reach for first.”
      Shelf cues
      Task
      Compare generic forms and visual cues in a shelf activity.
      We observed
      What draws attention and what people expect a package to do.
      Sample prompt
      “What do you expect from this package?”

      What it changed

      Dispensing, handling, and shelf cues stayed separate, and what people did in each activity shaped the direction the team presented.

      06 · Outcome

      A packaging direction and a team presentation

      The team presented a packaging direction with physical prototype studies.

      What I’d carry forward

      1. Watch the routineThe space, the storage, and the surrounding steps belong in the research.
      2. Separate the testsHandling and dispensing need their own activities and prompts.
      3. Let each phase hand offThe visits set the needs, the needs set the tests, and the tests set the direction the team presented.
